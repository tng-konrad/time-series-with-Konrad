# Time Series with Konrad: episode 15
Semi- and self-supervised learning: when labels are scarce and the signal is free

KONRAD BANACHEWICZ
AUG 11, 2026

The dangerous assumptions are the ones you don't notice. For fourteen episodes, this series has quietly enjoyed a luxury: our supervision signal was free. In forecasting, the target arrives on its own — you predict tomorrow, wait a day, and the truth shows up in your inbox. No annotation budget required.

Then you switch from *what happens next* to *what is this* — is this heartbeat arrhythmic, is this vibration a failing bearing, is this engine faulty — and the free lunch ends. Sensors produce signal continuously, for nothing. Labels come from a technician, a cardiologist, a teardown inspection. In every industrial project I have seen, the ratio is brutal: oceans of unlabeled series, a puddle of labeled ones.

This episode is about what to do with the ocean. The core principle:

**Labels are expensive; unlabeled signal is nearly free — and with the right pretext, the data can supervise itself.**

We will climb the following ladder: supervised baselines (to know what we're fighting for), **masked reconstruction** (generative self-supervision), **contrastive learning** (self-supervision by comparison), then the semi-supervised family — **pseudo-labeling**, a **FixMatch-style** consistency loop (which will blow up in our faces, instructively), and **multi-task joint training**. As usual: the goal is not mathematical rigor, but practical understanding.

## The problem: 95% of your labels just left the building

Our dataset is **FordA**, a classic from the UCR archive: 3,601 training and 1,320 test recordings of automotive engine noise, each 500 time steps long, each labeled *fault* or *no fault*. It is a genuine industrial diagnosis problem, the classes are nearly balanced (so 50% accuracy is the coin-flip floor), and the series come pre-standardized.

> **[FIGURE — graph15-1.png: one engine-noise recording from each class, overlaid]**

Look at the plot for a moment. One of these engines is faulty. Can you tell which? Neither can I. Both classes are equally noisy squiggles — the fault signature lives somewhere in the *texture* of the oscillations, not in any feature the naked eye can grab. This is exactly the kind of problem where learned features earn their keep, and where the cost of learning them from labels becomes economically interesting.

To simulate reality, we keep a stratified **5% of the training labels — 180 examples** — and lock the remaining 3,421 labels in a drawer. Every method in this episode plays by the same rules: use all 3,601 raw series if you like, but only 180 labels.

First, the goalposts, honestly earned:

```
{'1-NN (100% labels)': 0.665,
 '1-NN (5% labels)': 0.585,
 'supervised CNN (100% labels)': 0.932,
 'supervised CNN (5% labels)': 0.799}
```

Read them bottom-up. A dilated convolutional network with all labels reaches **93.2%** — our ceiling. The same network with 180 labels: **79.9%**. That thirteen-point gap is the villain of this episode.

Two asides worth their space. First, the humble **1-nearest-neighbour** classifier — find the most similar training series, copy its label — gets 66.5% with zero training. Any deep method that can't beat it is a result about wasted electricity. Second, a *pro tip* hiding in that 79.9%: we trained the small-label baseline for **100 epochs**, not the 40 the full-data model got. Epochs are not gradient steps — one pass over 180 examples is six steps, not twenty-nine — and an undertrained baseline sits near chance, which makes every fancy method look miraculous. Under-cooked baselines are one of the quiet ways label-efficiency papers flatter themselves. We gave ours room to converge; the gap is real.

## Teaching without a teacher

Self-supervised learning (SSL) attacks the gap in two acts. **Act one:** invent a *pretext task* whose answer key is generated by the data itself — no human required — and train an encoder on the full unlabeled pool. **Act two:** transplant that encoder under a small classification head and spend your precious 180 labels only there.

The literature sorts pretext tasks into three families: **generative** (hide part of the input, reconstruct it), **contrastive** (create multiple views of a series, learn to tell which views belong together), and **adversarial** (GAN-style generator-vs-discriminator games — rarer in practice, and we'll leave them as a pointer). We'll climb the first two.

And how do we grade a pretrained encoder? Two standard exams, used throughout:

- **Linear probe:** freeze the encoder, train only a logistic regression on its embeddings. With ~65 parameters, it can't overfit 180 labels — so its accuracy measures how *linearly separable* the classes already are in the learned space. Pure representation quality.
- **Fine-tuning:** put a head on the encoder and let *all* weights adapt, at a gently reduced learning rate (a tenth of the default — large steps would let 180 noisy gradients bulldoze the pretrained weights, a failure mode with the excellent name *catastrophic forgetting*).

## Rung one: masked reconstruction

The first pretext is the time series cousin of how BERT learned language: hide pieces of the input, demand they be filled back in. The "label" for a hidden stretch is the original signal — which we had all along.

> **[FIGURE — graph15-2.png: the masked pretext — original series vs. what the model sees, five 25-step holes]**

We hide five contiguous 25-step patches per series (roughly 25% of it) and train an encoder-decoder to minimize

$$L = \frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{t \in M} (x_t - \hat{x}_t)^2$$

In plain English: *mean squared error, counted where the holes are.* The model reconstructs the whole series, but at visible positions the task is trivial copying — all the learning pressure comes from the holes.

Why patches and not scattered single points? Because time series are locally smooth: an isolated missing point can be filled by linear interpolation between its neighbours, no understanding required. A 25-step hole cannot. **The difficulty of the pretext task is a design parameter** — too easy and the encoder learns shortcuts that transfer nothing; too hard and the task degenerates into guessing. We test this claim rather than cite it: probing a point-masked encoder gives 70.9% vs 72.4% for patch masking. Direction as advertised, margin modest — engine noise wiggles so fast that even single points aren't trivially interpolable. On smooth series (temperatures, demand), the gap is far wider; patch masking is the safe default.

*Pro tip:* your encoder's **receptive field must be wider than the holes**. Our first attempt used three plain convolutions — receptive field of 7 steps, staring into 25-step holes it physically could not see across. Reconstruction error nearly tripled. Dilated convolutions (dilation 1, 4, 16 — the signature encoder of TS2Vec and friends) widen the field to ~43 steps and fix it. Architecture and pretext have to be designed *together*.

> **[FIGURE — graph15-3.png: masked-autoencoder reconstruction on an unseen test series, hidden patches shaded]**

Read the plot: outside the shaded holes, the reconstruction hugs the original almost perfectly (it's copying). Inside them, it recovers the entry, the exit, and a *damped* version of the swings between — many continuations are consistent with the context, the MSE loss predicts something like their average, and averaging shrinks oscillations. Imperfect, and fine: we never wanted a signal-restoration tool. The question is whether the features built to fill holes also separate faulty from healthy.

The verdict, and this episode's first honest disappointment:

```
{'masked AE + linear probe (5%)': 0.724,
 'masked AE + fine-tune (5%)': 0.777}
```

Both *below* the 79.9% supervised baseline. All that pretraining, and we would have been better off ignoring it. :-/

Before you close the tab: this is a legitimate, literature-consistent result, not a bug. Reconstruction rewards retaining *everything* about the signal — class-relevant or not — which makes it the pretext of choice for forecasting and imputation, but gives it no reason to organize instances into separable clusters. Can we do better?

## Rung two: contrastive learning

Yes we can. The fix follows directly from the diagnosis: if reconstruction fails because it doesn't shape the *geometry* of the embedding space, pick a pretext that does nothing but shape geometry.

**Contrastive learning** trains by comparison. Take a series, make two randomly distorted *views* of it — a **positive pair** — and demand the encoder map them close together while views of *different* series land far apart. No pixel-level target at all; the supervisory signal is pure identity: "these two came from the same recording."

The raw material is data augmentation, and for time series that is a loaded decision. Flipping a photo of a cat still shows a cat; rescaling an ECG changes its medical meaning. Choosing augmentations *injects assumptions about what doesn't matter* — choose wrongly and you actively teach the encoder to ignore the signal you need. Following the TS-TCC recipe, we define two strengths:

> **[FIGURE — graph15-4.png: weak augmentation — jitter + scaling, the shape survives]**

> **[FIGURE — graph15-5.png: strong augmentation — segment permutation + jitter, local texture survives, global shape does not]**

The weak view (noise + amplitude scaling) is a plausible re-measurement of the same engine. The strong view chops the series into eight segments and shuffles them — locally intact, globally scrambled. For pretraining we pair two *weak* views; the strong one gets its moment later, with consequences.

The engine is the **NT-Xent loss** (a.k.a. InfoNCE). For an embedding $z_i$ whose positive partner is $z_j$, among $2B$ candidates in the batch:

$$\ell_i = -\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}$$

In plain English: *a classification problem where the correct answer is "your partner" and every other series in the batch is a wrong answer.* The temperature knob $\tau$ controls focus:

- `High τ`: all negatives count equally — soft, diffuse pressure.
- `Low τ`: the softmax concentrates on the *hardest* negatives — the near-lookalikes — which is where the learning signal lives. (We use 0.1.)

Did it work? Before any scores, look at what the encoder learned — these are test-set embeddings projected to 2-D, colored by true labels *the encoder never saw*:

> **[FIGURE — graph15-6.png: PCA of the raw series — classes fully entangled]**

> **[FIGURE — graph15-7.png: PCA of the contrastive embeddings — class structure emerges without labels]**

In raw-signal space, one undifferentiated cloud — the geometric restatement of why 1-NN struggles. In embedding space, the classes occupy visibly different regions with a contested frontier. Nobody told the encoder that classes exist. It was only asked to make recordings recognizable across noise and gain — and the features that do that turn out to correlate with the fault. **Class structure emerges as a by-product.** That is the quiet magic of self-supervision, and also its fine print: it happens only insofar as the pretext's notion of "same" aligns with your task's.

```
{'contrastive + linear probe (5%)': 0.913,
 'contrastive + fine-tune (5%)': 0.923}
```

With 180 labels, we are within a point of the 93.2% full-label ceiling. Even the frozen encoder plus a 65-parameter logistic regression demolishes the from-scratch CNN. The division of labor the literature keeps finding — **contrastive pretraining wins at classification, masked pretraining wins at forecasting** — shows up right on schedule.

## Semi-supervised, part one: the model labels its own data

Self-supervision never touched a label until the last step. **Semi-supervised** methods mix both kinds of data inside one process — and the simplest version sounds almost impertinent. Our mediocre 79.9% baseline model will *itself* annotate the 3,421 unlabeled series, and we will train on its guesses.

Mechanism:

1. Train a teacher on the 180 real labels.
2. Predict on the unlabeled pool.
3. Keep only predictions made with confidence ≥ 0.95 — these become **pseudo-labels**.
4. Retrain a fresh student on real + pseudo-labels.

The confidence threshold is the load-bearing wall. Even a mediocre classifier is far better than its average on the examples it is *sure* about:

```
pool size 3421, accepted at conf>=0.95: 1788
audit: accepted pseudo-labels are 97.5% correct (vs 78.6% for ALL pseudo-labels)
```

(That audit peeks at the hidden labels — a luxury of simulation. In production you can't check, which is why the threshold matters so much: too low floods training with garbage, too high accepts nothing. And note a subtler bite: the accepted set is class-imbalanced (701 vs 1,087), because the teacher is more confident about one class. Confidence filtering silently reshapes your training distribution.)

The payoff:

```
{'pseudo-labeling (5%)': 0.892}   # teacher was 0.799
```

Nine points, no new human labels, no new architecture, twenty lines of code. Pound for pound the best-value trick in this episode.

## Semi-supervised, part two: the flywheel that spins backwards

If one round of self-training works, surely iterating works better? And surely we should use our strong augmentation, FixMatch-style: harvest pseudo-labels on the *weak* view (where the class is evident), then make the student honour them on the *strong* view too — a consistency requirement that forces the model to find class evidence in local texture. Each round's student becomes the next round's teacher. Better teacher → more confident labels → more data → better student. A flywheel.

Here is what the flywheel actually did:

```
round 1: accepted 1320 pseudo-labels (91.9% correct), test accuracy 0.821
round 2: accepted 2721 pseudo-labels (64.3% correct), test accuracy 0.567
round 3: accepted 3375 pseudo-labels (52.0% correct), test accuracy 0.516
```

Round 1 behaves. Then acceptance *rises* while accuracy *collapses* — by round 3 the student confidently labels nearly the whole pool at coin-flip quality, and scores 51.6% on test. Below 1-NN. Below everything.

This is **confirmation bias**, the standing danger of all self-training, live and in colour: a student trained partly on shuffled, distorted copies became *confidently wrong* on part of the pool; the threshold happily accepted its confident mistakes; the next student learned them as truth and minted new ones. **Acceptance going up while quality drains away is the exact signature to memorize** — and in production, the reason to keep a small labeled validation set (affordable even when training labels are not) watching the trajectory. The literature's fixes — per-class adaptive thresholds (FlexMatch), uncertainty-aware selection, slowly-moving EMA teachers (Mean Teacher) — all target precisely this spiral. And the deeper echo of the contrastive section: strong augmentation is a powerful regularizer *only if it preserves what the labels mean.* Ours, evidently, did not preserve quite enough.

## Semi-supervised, part three: one model, two jobs

The last method dissolves the two-phase structure entirely. Give one shared encoder two heads — a classifier and a masked-reconstruction decoder — and optimize both at once:

$$L = L_{\text{supervised}} + \lambda \cdot L_{\text{auxiliary}}$$

All 3,601 series flow through the reconstruction head; only the 180 labeled ones contribute classification gradient (in Keras, a `sample_weight` of zero turns the unlabeled rows into arithmetic ghosts for the classifier while they keep teaching the encoder). The auxiliary task acts as a *continuous regularizer*: at every step, the encoder must satisfy both the label signal from 180 examples and the reconstruction signal from 3,601, which blocks the memorize-the-180 solution.

The knob is λ:

- `High λ`: the auxiliary task hijacks the encoder — you've built an autoencoder with a classification hobby.
- `Low λ`: the regularization vanishes and you're back to overfitting.

```
{'multi-task joint (5%)': 0.820}   # λ = 0.5, untuned
```

Two points over the baseline, and ahead of the two-stage masked-AE pipeline (77.7%) — the same reconstruction pretext helps more as a continuous regularizer than as a pretraining phase. Still far behind contrastive. A consistent story is forming.

Three honest observations. **One:** this ranking is a single draw of a noisy process — a different seed or label subset can reshuffle neighbours a point apart; trust the clusters, not the exact order. **Two:** don't over-generalize how completely the gap closed — FordA's classes differ in exactly the local texture contrastive learning preserves; on subtler problems expect unlabeled data to close *part* of the gap. Anyone promising that unlabeled data is always as good as labels is selling something. **Three:** the bottom row deserves a long look. A sophisticated method gone wrong finished below *no learning at all*.

## When is any of this worth the trouble?

One last experiment. We rerun three strategies — from-scratch supervised, pretrain + fine-tune, pretrain + linear probe — at three label budgets: 1%, 5%, 20%. One label-free pretraining serves all budgets; only the cheap final stage repeats.

> **[FIGURE — graph15-8.png: label-efficiency curve — three strategies vs. share of labels kept, with the full-label ceiling]**

```
1% labels (36 series):  scratch 0.516, fine-tune 0.500, probe 0.806
5% labels (180 series): scratch 0.799, fine-tune 0.923, probe 0.913
20% labels (720 series): scratch 0.909, fine-tune 0.930, probe 0.917
```

At 5% and 20%, fine-tuning rides within a point of the ceiling. But look at 1%: from-scratch *and fine-tuning both collapse to the coin flip* — 36 examples cannot train a fresh softmax head, let alone adapt 26,000 weights and their BatchNorm statistics — while the 65-parameter probe on the very same frozen encoder scores 80.6%, roughly what from-scratch training needs five times the labels to reach.

**The fewer labels you have, the fewer parameters you can afford to touch.** That single rule compresses the whole plot: below a few percent, freeze and probe; in the middle band, fine-tune (it's the difference between unusable and near-ceiling); past ~20%, the from-scratch model closes in and the pretraining premium shrinks toward noise — at which point the extra pipeline may no longer pay its rent.

## Closing time

We climbed from a thirteen-point label-scarcity gap through two families of self-supervision and three flavours of semi-supervision. Masked reconstruction taught us that a pretext can succeed *as a task* and still fail *as a pretraining* — it preserves everything and organizes nothing. Contrastive learning, which optimizes embedding geometry directly, closed the gap almost entirely. Pseudo-labeling delivered nine free points with twenty lines of code; its iterated cousin showed us confirmation bias eating a model alive; multi-task training turned the failed pretext into a serviceable regularizer.

The throughline: **unlabeled data is not a substitute for labels — it is a substitute for *most* of them**, provided the self-supervision you choose aligns with the question you're asking. And everything here is the simplest honest version of its idea: the heavily-engineered descendants are named TS2Vec, TS-TCC and its semi-supervised extension CA-TCC, TF-C (which adds a frequency-domain view — natural for signals like FordA), PatchTST and SimMTM on the masked side. Scaled to millions of series, masked pretraining is how the foundation models of the *previous episode* were built — "fine-tune a foundation model" is increasingly the strongest label-scarce baseline of all.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Benchmark the boring thing first:** 1-NN costs nothing and embarrasses any deep method that can't beat it — and a *properly trained* small-label baseline keeps you honest about your gains.
2. **Match the pretext to the task:** contrastive pretraining shapes geometry and wins at classification; masked reconstruction retains detail and wins at forecasting. Ours lost at classification exactly on schedule.
3. **Pretext difficulty is a design parameter:** patch masking beats point masking because interpolation is not understanding — and your encoder's receptive field must be wider than the holes.
4. **Pseudo-labeling is the highest benefit-to-effort trick in the book** — one round, high threshold, fresh student.
5. **Watch for the confirmation-bias signature:** acceptance rising while pseudo-label quality falls. Keep a small labeled validation set as your dashboard.
6. **The fewer labels, the fewer parameters you can afford to touch:** probe below a few percent, fine-tune in the middle, reconsider the whole pipeline once labels are plentiful. No free lunch — but a heavily discounted one.
